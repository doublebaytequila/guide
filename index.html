How Tequila is Made
From agave plant to bottle — the full journey
Step 1
Harvesting
Jimadores hand-harvest Blue Weber agave after 7–12 years of growth, cutting the piña (heart) from the plant. Each piña can weigh 40–90kg.
Step 2
Cooking
Piñas are slow-cooked in traditional brick ovens or autoclaves (pressure cookers) for 24–72 hours, converting starches to fermentable sugars.
Step 3
Extraction
Cooked agave is crushed to extract the juice (aguamiel). Traditional tahona stone wheels or modern roller mills are used — tahona is slower but gives more flavour.
Step 4
Fermentation
Agave juice ferments in wooden vats or steel tanks for 1–7 days as yeast converts sugars to alcohol, creating a low-ABV wash of around 4–7%.
Step 5
Distillation
Typically double-distilled in copper pot stills or stainless steel column stills to around 55% ABV, then diluted with water to bottling strength (usually 38–40%).
Step 6
Ageing
Depending on the category, tequila rests in oak barrels from a few months to several years. Some goes straight to bottle as Blanco.
The Categories
What the different styles actually mean
Blanco
Unaged (0–2 months)
Bottled immediately after distillation or rested briefly in stainless steel. The purest expression of agave — what you recommend to someone who wants to taste the actual spirit.
Reposado
2–11 months in oak
Rested in oak barrels — the legal minimum is 2 months, but quality producers go much longer. Picks up vanilla and caramel while keeping agave character. The most versatile category.
Añejo
1–3 years in oak
Aged in barrels under 600 litres. Deeper colour, richer flavour — oak, vanilla and dried fruit dominate. Great for customers who enjoy whisky or brandy.
Extra Añejo
3+ years in oak
A category introduced in 2006. Deep amber colour, complex and whisky-like. The most premium sipping category — recommend to whisky lovers or special occasion buyers.
Gold / Joven
Unaged blend
A blanco mixed with a small amount of aged spirit (or caramel colouring) for colour and softness. Typically the lowest quality category — best used for mixing only.
The Additives Issue
Why AMM community scores sometimes differ wildly from marketing

Mexican tequila law permits up to 1% by volume of additives without any disclosure requirement on the label. This is why a tequila can legally claim "100% agave" while still containing flavouring agents. The AMM community rates the liquid in the glass, so fake vanilla, sweetener, glycerin-smoothness and shortcut production usually get punished.

Tequilas widely suspected or confirmed to use additives include Casamigos, 818, Cincoro, Don Julio, Clase Azul Plata and many other well-known premium brands. Additive-free producers include Fortaleza, Tequila Ocho, El Cristiano, La Gritona and Cazcabel. A beautiful bottle or celebrity name does not fix thin agave character.

Glycerin
Adds a thick, oily mouthfeel and perceived smoothness. The most commonly used additive — gives that "creamy" texture many popular brands are known for.
Caramel Colour
Deepens the colour to make a tequila look more aged than it is. Used widely in Gold/Joven and some reposados.
Vanilla Extract
Adds sweet vanilla notes without the time needed to develop them naturally in oak. A telltale sign is vanilla that seems too prominent for the age stated.
Oak Extract
Mimics barrel ageing flavours — smoky, woody notes — without the time or cost of actual ageing. Common in cheaper aged expressions.
What is the AMM Score?
Understanding Agave Matchmaker ratings

Agave Matchmaker (AMM) is an independent community platform where enthusiasts and experts blind-taste and rate agave spirits. It's widely regarded as the most honest scoring system for tequila because it's free from brand sponsorship or marketing influence.

Each spirit receives a Panel score (from expert tasters) and a Community score (from the wider public). The scores shown in this guide are the Community scores — a useful real-world indicator of how a tequila actually drinks.

A score of 86+ is considered excellent. 80–85 is good. 70–79 is average. Below 70 indicates the community finds it lacking — often due to additives, diffuser production or poor agave quality. N/R means the brand isn't yet in the AMM database.

Distillery Family Tree
Many brands you think are separate actually share the same distillery — a great talking point with customers
NOM 1416
Casa Tradición
Jesús María, Los Altos, Jalisco
Clase Azul Plata Casamigos Avión
Three of the most famous celebrity and luxury brands — Clase Azul, George Clooney's Casamigos, and Avión — all come out of the same distillery in Jesús María. The hand-painted Clase Azul bottle is made nearby in Santa María Canchesdé.
NOM 1119
Casa Cuervo (La Rojeña)
Tequila, Jalisco — est. 1758
Jose Cuervo Especial Jose Cuervo Tradicional Jose Cuervo Reserva 1800 Tequila Maestro Dobel
La Rojeña is Latin America's oldest active distillery, operating since 1758. The Beckmann family (Cuervo descendants) also owns 1800 and Maestro Dobel — all produced under the same roof despite their very different price points and branding.
NOM 1438
Destiladora del Valle de Tequila
Tequila, Jalisco
Cincoro Don Julio
Don Julio and Michael Jordan's Cincoro are both produced at the same Jalisco distillery. Don Julio González founded his operation here in 1942 — the brand is now owned by Diageo.
NOM 1111
Hacienda Patrón
Atotonilco el Alto, Jalisco
Patrón Silver Patrón Reposado Patrón Añejo Patrón El Alto Patrón El Cielo
Hacienda Patrón is one of the most visited distilleries in Jalisco. It uniquely uses both traditional tahona stone wheels and modern roller mills — the El Alto and El Cielo expressions are tahona-exclusive, which is why they score better on AMM.
NOM 1493
Casa Herradura (Brown-Forman)
Amatitán, Jalisco — est. 1870
Herradura Reposado Herradura Añejo Herradura Ultra El Jimador
Casa Herradura invented the reposado category in 1974 and the extra añejo in 1962. El Jimador is their budget brand — same distillery, but switched to diffuser extraction to cut costs, which is why AMM scores dropped sharply.
NOM 1530
Tequila Fortaleza
Town of Tequila, Jalisco — est. 1999 (restored)
Fortaleza Blanco Fortaleza Reposado Fortaleza Añejo
The Sauza family (of Sauza Tequila fame) sold their brand but kept the original distillery. Guillermo Erickson Sauza restored it in 1999 and produces Fortaleza entirely on-site using a 100-year-old stone tahona — explaining why it's the highest-rated brand in our range.
NOM 1579
San Nicolás (Campari Group)
Los Altos, Jalisco
Espolón Blanco Espolón Reposado Espolón Añejo Altos Plata Altos Reposado
Espolón and Altos — both owned by Campari Group — are made at the same acclaimed San Nicolás distillery in the Los Altos highlands. Both consistently perform well with bartenders worldwide despite being mid-range priced.
NOM 1436
Destiladora González Bañuelos
Arandas, Los Altos, Jalisco
Cazcabel Blanco Cazcabel Reposado Cazcabel Añejo
Cazcabel is produced by UK-based Proof Drinks at this Highlands distillery. One of the better-value brands in the range — the community scores reflect genuine quality, with the blanco being particularly well-regarded.
NOM 1499

Jalisco
El Cristiano Blanco El Cristiano Reposado El Cristiano Extra Añejo
One of the hidden gems in the range. El Cristiano's Extra Añejo scores 87 on AMM — making it the joint-highest rated expression we stock alongside Tequila Ocho Plata. Unknown to most customers but worth every cent.
